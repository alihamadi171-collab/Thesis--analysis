# E3 — Khaled, "Abu Muhammad" (M, camp; table age 50; K6 = 12)
Demographic note: says he is "49 and some" (C16) and "about [4]9" (C256); later describes himself as 50 (C383, C398). Same borderline issue as E2; table not changed.
K6 note: the interviewer explained the scale as 1 = never … 4 = often (C26–C29), unlike the 0–4 scale used elsewhere. The participant's answers (sometimes / never / sometimes / never / sometimes / never) give 12 on that 1–4 coding but would be 6 on the standard 0–4 coding. The tabled K6 = 12 is therefore not directly comparable with other participants. Flag for researcher; K6 used descriptively only.
Narrative arc: In Syria a self-employed worker and driver (taxi, Suzuki pickup), formerly in police/security service, trained in secretarial computer skills in Damascus ("I could type with my eyes closed", C175–C179), electrician since childhood. Work meant independence, "master of himself" (C109–C116). In Jordan: farm guard, municipal street cleaner in Mafraq, intermittent NGO guard jobs; now not working (C79).
Role shift: the most vivid statement of infantilisation through aid dependence — "I felt I had become like my young son or daughter", both living off the same WFP card, "dependants of the organisations" (C127–C131).
Regard: contradictory — first says regard has not changed for him personally (C140), then: "Khaled has money, a car…" became "Khaled isn't worth a franc" (C143); society "loves the one who has money" (C136–C138). Notes that the questions relieve "the pain that I sometimes hide" (C149).
Exploitation: farm guard job, 6 am – 2 am, could not eat; endured three months "because of need" (C233–C243); could not complain because he had no work permit and no copy of the contract (C247–C251). Strong illustration of BAR-LEGAL producing WORK-PRECARIOUS.
Barriers: no university degree (C191–C208) despite teaching his son Tawjihi maths; wasta (C219, C223); too many applicants for few vacancies (C210–C214).
Negative case on age: repeatedly and firmly denies age-based exclusion (C212–C214, C258–C260, C267, C285, C292, C482) and says a fifty-year-old "is still a young man" equal to a 30–40-year-old (C423–C424). Yet he anticipates bodily decline ("maybe I won't be able to carry a bundle of bread", C345; "tomorrow, how will I live?", C348) and calls himself "too old for security" (C396). So age appears as an anticipated, internalised limit rather than an experienced external exclusion.
Dignity: distinguishes dignity (preserved — "you are still a free person", C296) from psychological comfort (affected) — a useful nuance against assuming that unemployment automatically damages dignity. Distress tied to not being able to give his son 10 qirsh or buy bread (C303–C305).
Strength: "willpower — even if I have to crawl… even if I'm disabled I'll open a small kiosk" (C359); God first, then children (C350–C352).
Future: "here in Jordan there is no future" (C367); return to Syria = return to his previous life, driving, self-confidence returning (C379–C390). Generation has "great" capacity to rebuild economically (skills) and socially (madafa gatherings, reconciliation, teaching war-raised youth to work, C420–C457).
Rights: "restore the dignity of the person"; do not give cash (50 or 100 JD "is like doing nothing"), give job vacancies and small projects so people feel self-reliant (C468, C489–C497). Notes those with many uneducated daughters and no sons face humiliation (C477–C480).
Reflexive note: interviewer repeatedly re-asked about age discrimination (C252, C277–C284, C481) — participant held his position; interviewer also offered "because the officials don't preserve the dignity of this age" (C483), which the participant endorsed — treat C484 as agreement with a researcher formulation.
