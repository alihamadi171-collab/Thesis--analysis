# E8 — Abu Abdo (M, 57, urban Amman; employed per table — CONFIRMED: working for about a month in a friend's bag shop (C124, C280–C282, C398); K6 = 13)
Narrative arc: From Aleppo (shoe-maker by trade, shoe workshop/showroom, Suzuki pick-up; land in Aleppo, C139–C168). Unusual pathway: he had already worked in Jordan for 20+ years before the war as an itinerant clothes seller ("dawaj"), mainly to Egyptian migrant workers in Al-Baqa'a (C153, C296–C306); brought his family from Syria by car at the start of the war (C207). Wife died; became "father and mother"; remarried; eight (later says eleven) children (C209–C219, C805). Two sons deported to Syria "for no reason", one conscripted, both detained; later one reached Germany (C228–C241). Opened a licensed shop — lost 10,000 JD after hospital/funeral costs and closure (C258–C265); creditors forgave half the debts; saved for Hajj with a friend's help (C265–C270). Itinerant trade collapsed after COVID (customers' purchasing power fell) (C384–C397); then a year and a half without work (C126, C156) — "a turning point in my life" (C488). Now selling in a friend's bag shop for less than a month (C282).
Dignity: physical tasks in work do not touch dignity; being ordered to make tea for someone does (C417–C418) — a nuanced boundary. Would sit two days without food rather than ask a neighbour for bread (C215, C252). Being given unasked-for help vs being refused after asking (C514–C522).
Unemployment at home: "the first thing you lose when sitting at home is your moral worth in the house"; constant bickering; going out to work "cuts 60–70% of the problems"; children kiss your hand when you return with money (C431–C444). He divorced his wife at one point because of the quarrelling (C496); reports neighbours where wives beat or divorced husbands who sat at home without income (C451–C462). Strongest case linking unemployment to family conflict and loss of male household authority.
Regard: no material differences among refugees — "we are all refugees", so regard has not changed (C252, C573) — negative case for REGARD-CHANGE.
Age: denies age exclusion; never applied for unsuitable things — "I respected myself" (C365–C403). But: "nobody employs a 60-year-old even if competent" (C589–C593); feels his brain cells are "used up", wife calls him senile (C649–C658). Older refugees "consumed like a Pepsi can" (C610).
Programs: welcomes machines plus follow-up — follow-up means "people care about you… you have value" (C479–C481).
Faith: central — "every time I said 'O Lord' I found Him" (C499–C536); faith narratives (gifts from strangers, forgiven debts) structure his account of coping.
Future: "In Jordan I put my ideas in the fridge and threw it at the South Pole" — only return or resettlement (C543); first need housing in Syria, then a modest shoe shop (C548–C556). Generation's economic role depends on capital; 80% have houses in Syria (C565–C584). Social role: guide youth back to God; "come down to their level, don't expect them to rise to yours" (C781–C794).
Rights: prioritise widows, the sick, those who raised many children — "they need to feel they are still human beings, not dead" (C803–C808).
Reflexive note: C690–C725 interviewer introduced an extended hypothetical about killing/sectarian revenge and a personal exchange about the interviewer's own thesis — a long digression led by the researcher; responses there are about religious ethics and should be used cautiously. Interviewer acknowledges "this question is extra… I added it" (C701, C775).
